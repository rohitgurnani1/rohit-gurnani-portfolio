I've always been a little obsessed with good displays.

Not necessarily the biggest screen or the one with the highest resolution, but the kind of display that makes you stop for a second and appreciate how good something looks.

I currently use a 13-inch M5 MacBook Air alongside a 55-inch Samsung Odyssey Ark. The Air has an IPS LCD, while the Ark uses Quantum Mini-LED technology.

Two completely different displays, sitting on the same desk.

And lately, with Apple introducing its new PROLED XDR OLED display in the redesigned MacBook Pro, I've been getting even more curious about where display technology is heading.

Apple is calling it the world's best laptop display, and after reading through the technical paper they published, I can see why they're making such a big deal about it.

But before we get into Apple's latest engineering, I think it's worth understanding what actually makes these displays different.

Why does Mini-LED look so much better than a traditional LCD? Why are people so obsessed with OLED? And what makes an OLED display genuinely better than another OLED?

The answer is a lot more interesting than just brighter colors.

It comes down to how these displays produce and control light.

## It All Starts With Light

Every display is trying to accomplish essentially the same thing: control light precisely enough to create an image.

But the way different displays do this is surprisingly different.

An LCD doesn't actually produce light through its pixels. Instead, it relies on a backlight behind a layer of liquid crystals.

Those crystals control how much light passes through each pixel.

OLED works differently. Each pixel generates its own light.

This sounds like a relatively small distinction, but it fundamentally changes how a display handles brightness, black levels, contrast, and even motion.

And once you understand this difference, a lot of the marketing terminology starts making sense.

## IPS: The Reliable Workhorse

IPS stands for In-Plane Switching.

It's a type of LCD technology that has been around for decades and is widely used in laptops, monitors, and professional displays.

My M5 MacBook Air uses an LED-backlit IPS display.

And honestly, it's a really good screen.

The text is sharp, colors look accurate, viewing angles are excellent, and Apple's color management makes the whole experience feel polished.

But IPS has an inherent limitation.

Even when an image is supposed to be completely black, the backlight behind the LCD is still producing light.

The liquid crystals try to block that light, but they cannot block it perfectly.

That's why blacks on many IPS displays can appear slightly gray, especially in a dark room.

If you've ever watched a movie with dark scenes on a regular LCD, you might have noticed this.

A dark nighttime scene doesn't look completely black. There's a faint glow across the display.

This also affects contrast.

### What exactly is contrast?

Contrast ratio describes the difference between the brightest white and darkest black a display can produce.

For example, a display with a 1,000:1 contrast ratio can produce a white that's 1,000 times brighter than its black level under the measurement conditions.

The greater that difference, the more depth an image can appear to have.

A good IPS screen can reproduce beautiful colors while still having relatively modest contrast.

And that's an important distinction.

**Color accuracy and contrast are not the same thing.**

A screen can reproduce a particular shade of red very accurately while still struggling to make black look truly black.

## Mini-LED: The Clever Way to Improve LCD

This brings me to my Samsung Odyssey Ark.

It's a 55-inch curved 4K monitor with Quantum Mini-LED backlighting, and it's probably the most ridiculous screen I've ever had on my desk.

The interesting part isn't just its size.

It's how it controls light.

Traditional LCD displays typically use a comparatively simple backlight arrangement.

Mini-LED replaces that with a large number of much smaller LEDs, arranged into independently controlled zones.

This allows something called *local dimming*.

Imagine watching a scene of stars against a dark night sky.

On a conventional IPS LCD, the backlight is still illuminating much of the screen, making it difficult to achieve deep blacks.

On a Mini-LED display, the monitor can dim the backlight in darker regions while keeping the stars bright.

My second-generation Odyssey Ark has 1,056 local dimming zones.

That's a substantial amount of lighting control compared with a basic LCD.

And the result can be a much more dramatic image.

Bright highlights stand out, shadows appear deeper, and HDR content can have considerably more impact.

One technical distinction: my Ark uses a VA LCD panel with Mini-LED backlighting, not IPS. Mini-LED describes the backlight technology and can be combined with different types of LCD panels.

But there's a catch.

### The problem with blooming

A Mini-LED dimming zone doesn't necessarily control just one pixel.

It may illuminate thousands of pixels at once.

So when a tiny bright object appears against a completely black background, the display may need to illuminate an entire zone to show that object.

Some of that light can affect the surrounding area.

This creates a glowing halo called *blooming*.

You might notice it around white subtitles, a mouse cursor on a black screen, or stars in a dark scene.

The better the display's dimming algorithm and zone arrangement, the less noticeable it becomes.

Mini-LED is an impressive engineering solution, but it's still fundamentally an LCD trying to control a backlight more precisely.

Which brings us to OLED.

## OLED: What If Every Pixel Controlled Its Own Light?

OLED stands for Organic Light-Emitting Diode.

Unlike IPS or Mini-LED LCDs, OLED doesn't require a separate backlight.

Each pixel produces its own light and can turn off completely.

Think about that for a second.

A 4K display contains approximately 8.3 million pixels.

On a 4K OLED, every one of those pixels can control its own light output.

Instead of controlling light through hundreds or thousands of backlight zones, you effectively get pixel-level control across the entire image.

That's why OLED is so good at displaying black.

When a pixel needs to be black, it simply turns off.

No backlight bleeding through. No dimming zone illuminating nearby pixels. No glowing halo around bright objects.

Just black.

And because the black level can approach zero, OLED can achieve an effectively infinite contrast ratio under ideal viewing conditions.

This is why OLED displays can look almost three-dimensional in dark scenes.

A bright object against a black background appears to stand out in a way that's difficult for traditional LCD technology to reproduce.

It's not necessarily that every color becomes more accurate.

It's that the relationship between light and darkness becomes much more precise.

And that changes how we perceive the entire image.

## Apple's PROLED XDR: What Happens When OLED Gets an Engineering Overhaul?

This is where things get really interesting.

On October 28, 2026, Apple announced its new MacBook Pro featuring the PROLED XDR display.

It's Apple's first OLED display in a Mac, and unlike some conventional OLED implementations, it uses a technology called **Tandem OLED**.

Apple published an entire 20-page technical paper explaining the engineering behind it.

I went through the paper, and what caught my attention wasn't just the brightness or contrast figures.

It was how many different engineering problems Apple is trying to solve simultaneously.

Because OLED, despite its beautiful image quality, has never been perfect.

### Two OLED layers instead of one

A conventional single-stack OLED relies on one main electroluminescent emitting stack to produce light.

Tandem OLED takes a different approach.

It stacks two light-emitting layers that work together to produce the image.

Why does that matter?

Because instead of making a single emitting layer work harder to achieve higher brightness, two layers can share the workload.

Each layer can operate at a lower current for a given target brightness.

In principle, this offers several benefits:

- Higher achievable brightness.
- Improved power efficiency.
- Less stress on individual emitters.
- Longer potential panel lifespan.

That's a pretty clever solution.

We're not just adding more brightness by forcing the display to consume more power.

We're changing how the light is generated in the first place.

Apple claims its PROLED XDR achieves up to 1,000 nits of sustained full-screen brightness and 2,000 nits of peak HDR brightness.

It can also reach 2,000 nits peak SDR brightness under very bright ambient conditions.

For a laptop OLED, those are seriously impressive numbers.

And I think they're particularly interesting because OLED has traditionally struggled with sustained brightness compared with high-end Mini-LED displays.

But peak brightness and full-screen sustained brightness are different measurements, so comparing displays requires understanding the conditions behind those numbers.

### A contrast ratio of 10,000,000:1

Apple specifies a contrast ratio of ten million to one.

That's an enormous number.

For perspective, a typical IPS LCD might have a native contrast ratio around 1,000:1, while high-end Mini-LED displays can achieve much greater dynamic contrast by dimming their backlights.

OLED changes the comparison because individual pixels can become effectively black.

Apple's specified ratio reflects its display characterization rather than a universal limit of OLED technology.

The most important part isn't really the number itself.

It's that bright pixels and dark pixels can exist directly beside one another without a shared backlight zone.

Imagine editing a nighttime photograph.

A tiny streetlight can remain bright while the surrounding shadows stay dark.

With Mini-LED, local dimming zones have to manage the competing demands of those bright and dark regions.

With OLED, each pixel controls its own emission.

That precision is what creates the dramatic contrast we associate with OLED.

### The part I found most fascinating: pixel-level compensation

Here's something I hadn't thought much about before reading Apple's technical paper.

OLED pixels don't all behave identically.

They can have small manufacturing variations, and their characteristics change as the organic materials age.

Temperature can also influence their behavior.

So even if you send the exact same electrical signal to two pixels, they might not produce precisely the same brightness or color over their entire lifetime.

Apple's approach involves a dedicated display engine integrated into its M5 Pro and M5 Max silicon.

This engine continuously adjusts the display based on several factors:

- What content is being displayed.
- How individual pixels have been used.
- The panel's temperature.
- Ambient lighting conditions.
- Pixel-level calibration data.

Apple describes these corrections as happening in real time.

It also uses what it calls a full-oxide transistor layer to control the current driving individual pixels more precisely.

This is where hardware and software engineering start becoming inseparable.

The quality of the display isn't determined only by the OLED materials.

It's also influenced by the algorithms, transistors, calibration data, and processing hardware controlling them.

I find that fascinating.

We're essentially using computation to compensate for the imperfections and aging characteristics of the physical display.

### What about OLED burn-in?

Burn-in is probably the biggest concern I would have about using OLED on a development machine.

As a software engineer, I spend hours looking at relatively static interfaces.

IDE sidebars, terminal windows, browser toolbars, menu bars, and code editors can stay in the same place for extended periods.

Since OLED uses organic emitters, those pixels gradually age with use.

If some areas age differently from others, the result can be uneven brightness or persistent image retention.

Apple says its new display engine monitors pixel usage and applies compensation to help mitigate long-term burn-in.

The tandem architecture may also help because each emitting layer can operate under less electrical stress.

That sounds promising.

But I'd still want to see long-term independent testing before declaring OLED burn-in a solved problem.

Engineering can reduce the risk without making organic materials immune to aging.

### Better colors aren't just about OLED

Apple also made an interesting decision with color reproduction.

The PROLED XDR supports both P3 and Adobe RGB color gamuts, with more than 80% coverage of Rec. 2020.

Why is that useful?

P3 is widely used in Apple's ecosystem and professional video workflows.

Adobe RGB is especially relevant to photography and print production because of its wider coverage in certain green and cyan regions.

Rec. 2020 defines a much wider color space used in modern HDR video standards.

Supporting a wider range of colors gives professionals greater flexibility, provided the display is also accurately calibrated.

And that last part matters.

A display capable of showing a huge range of colors isn't necessarily displaying them correctly.

Apple says each PROLED XDR panel undergoes precise factory calibration, with the display engine maintaining that accuracy over time.

For photographers, video editors, designers, and anyone working on color-sensitive content, that could be genuinely useful.

### Even the reflections are an engineering problem

Another detail from Apple's technical paper surprised me.

The standard PROLED XDR cover glass has a stated reflectance of just 1.00%.

Apple attributes this to improvements in both internal panel reflections and the external antireflective coating.

There's also an optional nano-texture glass finish designed to scatter reflections.

Why does this matter?

Because contrast isn't just about what happens inside the display.

It also depends on the environment.

A perfectly black OLED pixel can still appear gray if sunlight or room lighting reflects off the glass.

Reducing reflections helps preserve the appearance of deep blacks and rich colors in real-world conditions.

That's particularly important for laptops, which are constantly moving between different lighting environments.

### And it's fast

Apple says its OLED pixels switch in the microsecond range.

Compare that with LCD pixel transitions, which commonly take milliseconds.

That can substantially reduce pixel-transition blur and ghosting during fast motion.

The new display also supports ProMotion with an adaptive refresh rate ranging from 10Hz to 120Hz.

So you get fast pixel transitions alongside high refresh rates, with the ability to reduce refresh activity when the content doesn't require it.

For gaming, scrolling through code, navigating large documents, and working with animations, that's an appealing combination.

### Is it actually the world's best laptop display?

Apple certainly thinks so.

And on paper, PROLED XDR looks incredibly impressive.

The combination of tandem OLED, extreme brightness, pixel-level compensation, expanded color gamut, low reflectance, and high refresh rates addresses several limitations traditionally associated with OLED laptops.

But I think it's important to separate engineering claims from independently verified results.

A display can have remarkable specifications and still involve tradeoffs in battery consumption, long-term aging, color handling, or performance under certain conditions.

The new MacBook Pro isn't widely available until November 6, so I would reserve a definitive judgment until independent testing becomes available.

Still, I have to admit, this might be the most interesting laptop display Apple has introduced in years.

And as someone already considering moving from an M5 MacBook Air to a MacBook Pro, I'm particularly curious to experience the difference in person.

Not because my current IPS display is bad.

But because OLED is beginning to solve problems that once made Mini-LED the more practical choice for professional laptops.

## Why Colors Look So Different

Here's something I think gets misunderstood in a lot of display comparisons.

People often say OLED has better colors.

But that isn't universally true.

There are several different things that determine color quality.

**Color gamut** describes the range of colors a display can reproduce.

**Color accuracy** describes how closely the displayed colors match their intended values.

**Color volume** considers how well a display maintains colors across different brightness levels.

And **contrast** influences how those colors look next to bright and dark regions.

These are related, but they aren't interchangeable.

A professionally calibrated IPS display can be more color-accurate than a poorly calibrated OLED.

And high-end Quantum Dot Mini-LED displays can reproduce an impressively wide range of vivid colors.

My Odyssey Ark, for example, uses Quantum Dot technology along with Mini-LED backlighting.

So why does OLED often appear more vibrant?

Because our eyes perceive colors in relation to their surroundings.

A saturated blue against a truly black background can appear more striking than the exact same blue surrounded by a faint gray glow.

OLED's contrast makes colors feel richer, even when the underlying color values aren't necessarily more accurate.

That's a subtle but important distinction.

## What About HDR?

HDR, or High Dynamic Range, is where these display technologies become especially interesting.

HDR isn't simply about making the entire screen brighter.

It's about representing a greater range of brightness—from dark shadows to intense highlights—while preserving detail.

Imagine a nighttime city scene.

You want the sky to remain dark, but you also want streetlights, reflections, and illuminated signs to look convincingly bright.

An effective HDR display needs to manage both extremes.

Mini-LED is particularly impressive here because high-end models can produce extremely bright highlights and sustain substantial brightness across larger portions of the screen.

OLED excels at maintaining deep blacks right next to bright objects.

Apple's new tandem OLED is interesting because it attempts to combine OLED's pixel-level contrast with brightness capabilities that have traditionally been an advantage of premium Mini-LED panels.

Neither technology automatically wins every HDR comparison.

Display quality depends heavily on implementation, not just the technology name.

## There's Also Motion

Another advantage of OLED is pixel response time.

Refresh rate and response time are two different things.

Refresh rate tells us how frequently the display can present a new frame.

Response time describes how quickly individual pixels transition between states.

A display running at 120Hz updates its image up to 120 times per second.

But if the pixels take too long to change, moving objects can leave visible trails or smearing.

OLED pixels generally transition much faster than LCD pixels.

This can make fast-moving content look exceptionally crisp.

For gaming, that's a significant benefit.

However, fast pixel response doesn't mean a 60Hz OLED automatically feels smoother than a 165Hz Mini-LED monitor.

Refresh rate, response time, frame rate, and motion processing all contribute to the experience.

## So Why Doesn't Everything Use OLED?

If OLED is so good, why are we still buying IPS and Mini-LED displays?

There are some genuine tradeoffs.

The biggest long-term concern is burn-in.

Modern OLED displays use sophisticated compensation algorithms and other techniques to reduce uneven pixel aging.

Apple's new PROLED XDR is a particularly interesting example.

But even with these innovations, the long-term durability of a new panel needs to be demonstrated through actual use.

OLED can also be more expensive, and different OLED implementations vary in sustained brightness and efficiency.

Mini-LED doesn't have the same organic-pixel burn-in mechanism, which makes it appealing for desktop productivity.

And conventional IPS remains a mature, reliable, and often more affordable solution.

So there's a real argument for all three technologies.

## What I'd Actually Choose

Having used an IPS MacBook Air and a Mini-LED Odyssey Ark, I can appreciate why these displays exist.

The Air's IPS panel is excellent for everyday productivity. It's sharp, accurate, efficient, and doesn't require me to think much about burn-in.

The Odyssey Ark gives me a huge workspace, strong HDR capability, high refresh rates, and considerably more sophisticated control over backlighting.

And Apple's new PROLED XDR is probably the first laptop OLED that has me seriously interested in upgrading for the display itself.

Not just because it has OLED.

But because of the effort Apple has put into addressing the technology's limitations.

The tandem emitters, dedicated display engine, advanced calibration, high brightness and reflection control are genuinely interesting engineering decisions.

That said, I wouldn't necessarily replace every display I own with OLED.

For a large monitor displaying coding environments and static windows throughout the day, Mini-LED remains extremely compelling.

For a laptop that I use for development, photography, watching movies, and occasionally gaming, OLED is an attractive upgrade—especially when paired with high refresh rates and good brightness.

The question isn't really which technology is universally best.

It's which compromises make sense for how you use your computer.

## Final Thoughts

I find display engineering fascinating because we're solving a deceptively simple problem.

We're trying to control light.

But doing that accurately, millions of times across a screen, while maintaining brightness, color, efficiency, and durability is extraordinarily complicated.

IPS gives us a reliable and mature foundation.

Mini-LED takes LCD technology much further through sophisticated backlight control.

OLED approaches the problem differently by making every pixel its own light source.

And now, with technologies such as Apple's PROLED XDR, we're seeing how much further OLED can be pushed through materials science, transistor engineering, dedicated silicon, and intelligent compensation algorithms.

That's what I find most exciting.

The future of displays isn't just about producing brighter pixels or adding more resolution.

It's about how precisely we can control every individual pixel, how consistently we can reproduce colors, and how efficiently we can do all of this over the lifetime of a device.

Next time you look at a beautiful image on your screen, pay attention to the dark areas rather than just the bright colors.

Look at how black the blacks are, whether light bleeds into nearby regions, and how much detail you can see in the shadows.

Because sometimes, what makes a display impressive isn't how much light it produces.

**It's how precisely it knows when not to produce any light.**
