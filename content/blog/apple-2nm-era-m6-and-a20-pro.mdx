I've always found it fascinating how much computing power we can fit into something that barely weighs a few pounds.

Think about it. Not too long ago, getting serious performance meant having a desktop computer with a massive cooling system, a dedicated GPU, and enough fans to make your room sound like a server rack. Today, we're running local AI models, compiling large projects, editing 4K video, and playing some pretty demanding games on machines that can last an entire day on battery.

And somehow, we're still finding ways to make these chips smaller, faster, and more efficient.

Apple recently introduced its M6 and A20 Pro chips, marking its move to a new 2-nanometer manufacturing process. I've been following Apple Silicon pretty closely, and this is one of those developments that genuinely makes me excited about where personal computing is heading.

Not necessarily because I need another laptop. My M5 MacBook Air is doing just fine.

But because of what's happening underneath the hood.

## First, what does 2nm actually mean?

You've probably seen terms like 7nm, 5nm, 3nm, and now 2nm whenever a new processor gets announced.

For the longest time, I understood the general idea: smaller transistors meant better chips. But the engineering behind that is a lot more interesting.

A modern processor contains billions of transistors. These are microscopic electronic switches that turn on and off to represent and process information.

Every application we run, from a simple calculator to a large language model, ultimately depends on enormous numbers of these switches working together.

The manufacturing process determines how densely and efficiently those transistors can be built.

One important clarification: **2nm doesn't mean every transistor is literally two nanometers wide.** Modern process-node names are labels for generations of semiconductor technology, rather than exact physical transistor dimensions.

Still, moving from 3nm to 2nm is a meaningful engineering achievement.

A newer process can allow chip designers to fit more transistors into a given area, reduce the energy required for switching, improve performance, or balance all three.

And this is where things get interesting.

## Why smaller transistors matter

Imagine having a fixed amount of space to build a city.

You can either build the same number of houses using less land, or use that space to build more houses, roads, and infrastructure.

Chip design works somewhat similarly.

With greater transistor density, engineers can dedicate more silicon to CPU cores, GPU cores, cache, neural processing hardware, and other specialized components.

But transistor density isn't the entire story.

Every transistor consumes energy when switching, and real chips also lose energy through leakage. As processors get more complex, managing heat and power becomes increasingly difficult.

The goal isn't simply to squeeze more transistors onto a chip. It's to make those transistors perform useful work as efficiently as possible.

That's why I find performance per watt so fascinating.

A processor that completes a task 20% faster is impressive. But a processor that completes the same task while consuming substantially less energy can be even more useful, especially in a laptop or phone.

Less energy can mean less heat, less cooling, longer battery life, or more performance within the same power budget.

Of course, smaller process nodes don't automatically guarantee every one of these improvements. Architecture, voltage, clock speeds, chip packaging, and software optimization all play enormous roles.

## Enter the M6

Apple introduced the M6 in August 2026, and it's the company's first chip manufactured using a 2nm process.

The M6 features a 12-core CPU, 12-core GPU, and a dual 16-core Neural Engine.

For comparison, the M5 has a 10-core CPU and 10-core GPU.

Apple claims the M6 delivers up to 30% faster graphics performance and up to 25% faster performance in certain on-device AI workloads compared with the M5.

Those are meaningful generational improvements.

But what interests me more is how Apple is distributing computing power across different parts of the chip.

We no longer live in a world where the CPU handles everything.

Modern applications rely on several different kinds of processors working together.

The CPU handles general-purpose computation. The GPU is great at highly parallel workloads. Dedicated neural hardware accelerates supported machine-learning operations.

The M6 continues pushing this heterogeneous computing model, where different tasks can run on hardware specifically designed to execute them efficiently.

As someone who works with software and AI, I think this is becoming increasingly important.

The future of computing isn't necessarily about making one processor do everything faster. It's about getting the right kind of computation onto the right hardware.

## The A20 Pro might be just as interesting

Apple's A20 Pro takes the same 2nm transition into smaller devices.

The chip powers the iPhone 18 Pro lineup and the newly announced iPad mini.

Apple says the new iPad mini can deliver up to 2.7 times the graphics performance and up to 4.5 times the AI performance of the previous generation, depending on the workload.

That's a pretty remarkable jump for a device you can comfortably hold in one hand.

The A20 Pro also supports hardware-accelerated ray tracing, mesh shading, and neural acceleration.

What fascinates me is that we're approaching a point where a tablet can perform workloads that would have required a considerably larger computer not that long ago.

Of course, an iPad isn't suddenly a replacement for a high-end workstation. Memory capacity, cooling, software restrictions, and sustained performance still matter.

But the amount of computing power available in these tiny devices is incredible.

And with local AI becoming increasingly practical, that hardware could enable applications that don't need to send every request to a server.

Imagine more sophisticated offline assistants, real-time image processing, intelligent creative tools, and personalized applications that run directly on your device.

There's something really appealing about that from both a performance and privacy perspective.

## The part I'm most excited about: battery life

I currently use a 13-inch M5 MacBook Air with 24GB of unified memory.

It's genuinely one of my favorite computers I've owned.

Most of my work involves coding, experimenting with AI tools, running development environments, and occasionally working with Unity and other creative software.

And the fact that I can do all of this on a fanless machine is still pretty impressive to me.

But here's what I'm particularly curious about.

**What happens when Apple brings the M6 to the MacBook Air?**

The Air is already designed around efficiency. It doesn't have a fan, so it relies on passive cooling to manage heat.

That makes the efficiency improvements of a new manufacturing process especially interesting.

If the M6 can deliver the same workload using less power, the Air could potentially run cooler and maintain its performance longer.

And if Apple chooses to prioritize efficiency over peak performance, we could see meaningful improvements in real-world battery life.

I'm not talking about watching a video with the brightness turned down.

I'm talking about an actual day of development.

A few hours in Xcode, a browser full of documentation, terminal sessions, local services, maybe a small AI model running in the background, and still having enough battery left to work somewhere without hunting for an outlet.

That's the kind of improvement I'd love to see.

Of course, we'll need to wait for an M6 MacBook Air and independent testing before knowing how much of that potential translates into reality.

Apple could also use the efficiency gains to increase performance while keeping battery life similar.

Either outcome would be interesting.

## Why this matters even more in the age of AI

One thing I've been thinking about lately is how AI is changing what we expect from our computers.

For years, a lot of everyday software development was constrained primarily by the complexity of the code, build times, or how quickly we could work through a problem.

Now, developers can have multiple AI-assisted workflows running alongside their IDEs, browsers, databases, and other tools.

And local AI introduces an entirely different set of hardware requirements.

Running a language model isn't just about having a fast CPU.

Memory capacity matters. Memory bandwidth matters. GPU performance matters. And when you're running models for extended periods, power consumption and thermal management matter too.

This is why I'm interested in the combination of Apple's unified memory architecture and its increasingly capable GPUs and Neural Engines.

I don't think local models are going to replace cloud-based AI anytime soon. Large cloud models will continue to have advantages in capability and scale.

But I do think we'll see more hybrid applications where smaller, specialized models run locally while heavier tasks are handled in the cloud.

And as chips become more efficient, developers will have more freedom to experiment with those possibilities without requiring expensive dedicated hardware.

## We're getting close to some interesting physical limits

There's another side to this story that I think deserves attention.

As impressive as 2nm sounds, semiconductor manufacturing is becoming extraordinarily difficult.

At these scales, engineers have to deal with increasingly challenging problems involving electrical leakage, heat, manufacturing variation, interconnect resistance, and quantum effects.

And shrinking transistors doesn't necessarily make the wires connecting them faster.

In some cases, the interconnects and memory movement can become bigger bottlenecks than the transistors themselves.

That's why improvements in chip packaging, memory architecture, caching, and specialized accelerators are becoming so important.

We're not going to keep getting better computers simply by shrinking everything forever.

The next major breakthroughs might come just as much from how chips are designed and connected as from how small their transistors become.

## Final thoughts

I think it's easy to get caught up in benchmark scores whenever Apple announces a new chip.

And don't get me wrong, I love seeing those numbers too.

But what excites me most about the M6 and A20 Pro isn't just that they're faster.

It's that we're continuing to make powerful computing more accessible, portable, and energy-efficient.

A few years ago, running sophisticated AI workloads on a thin laptop would have sounded pretty ambitious. Today, it's something you can experiment with on a MacBook Air.

And we're only getting started.

I'm particularly looking forward to seeing what Apple does with an M6 MacBook Air. As someone already using an M5 Air, I'm less interested in whether the next generation opens apps a fraction of a second faster and more interested in what it can sustain on battery.

Because at the end of the day, the most impressive computer isn't always the one that draws the most power.

Sometimes it's the one that makes you forget how much engineering is happening underneath your fingertips.

And that's what makes this whole 2nm transition so exciting to me.
